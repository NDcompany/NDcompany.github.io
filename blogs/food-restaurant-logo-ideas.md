# Food logo ideas: what the logos of 50 big restaurant chains tell a small food business

Red is in 31 of the 50 biggest restaurant chains' logos, and 34 are lettering only. What that means for a cafe, restaurant or cloud kitchen logo, and a checklist before you approve one.

By [Nakshatra Ranjan Saha](https://ndcompany.in/team/nakshatra-ranjan-saha), CEO & Founder Published 7 November 2026 9 min read

The typical food logo among the 50 largest restaurant chains is red lettering that spells out the name, with no symbol and no mascot. Red appears in 31 of the 50 logos (62%), 34 are lettering only, and 47 spell out the full name. On 7 November 2026 we looked at the logo of each of the 50 chains with the most locations, measured the colours by software and judged the rest by eye. This post shows what we found, what it does and does not tell a small business, and which restaurant logo ideas are worth taking from it, with some of our own food and drink logos as examples.

Key takeaways

- Red is in 31 of 50 chains' logos (62%). 38 of 50 use red, orange or yellow. Red and yellow together are rare: only 3 chains.
- Simple colour schemes dominate: 22 chains use one colour, 16 use two and 12 use three.
- 34 of 50 are lettering only, 16 have a symbol or icon, and only 3 have a mascot or character.
- 47 of 50 spell out the full name. The exceptions are McDonald's (the arches), Dairy Queen (DQ) and Vkusno i Tochka.
- Lettering style is split almost evenly: 12 bold sans, 12 serif and 12 script or handwritten. There is no single style that food logos use.

## How we did the study

We took Wikipedia's [list of the largest fast food restaurant chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_largest_fast_food_restaurant_chains), which ranks chains by number of locations, and went down it in order, keeping the first 50 chains that have a logo on Wikidata. We downloaded each logo file from Wikimedia Commons on 7 November 2026. The colours were measured by software from the files. Everything else, meaning whether there is a symbol, a mascot, what the lettering style is and whether the name is spelled out, we judged by eye and recorded in a spreadsheet.

The limits matter. The Commons file is not always the chain's current logo: Taco Bell's file is an older sign, and Starbucks's is the wordmark without the siren. Chains that have no logo on Wikidata are missing, and Popeyes and Chipotle are two of them. Colour measuring gives the dominant colours, so a small accent can be missed. The sample is 50 large chains, most of them American, not the restaurants around the corner from you. We name the chains because they are public brands, and we show none of their logos.

## What colours do restaurant chain logos use?

Mostly red. Of the 50 logos, 31 contain red, and 38 contain red, orange or yellow, the warm colours that food brands are known for. White comes second, at 18.

Red and yellow together, the pairing many people picture first, appear in only 3 logos: McDonald's, Sukiya, and Checkers and Rally's. Most chains use one colour (22), or two (16). Only 12 use three. Even the biggest names keep the palette small.

By type of food, the burger chains lean red (9 of 14), pizza chains more so (6 of 8), and all 4 chicken chains use it. Coffee is less sure, with 2 of 4. These groups are small, so read them as a tendency in this sample, not a rule.

One thing the data cannot say is that red works. These are firms with large advertising budgets, and a logo that is on thousands of signs gets known whatever its colour. What the numbers do show is that red is the default, which is useful to know before you choose it.

## Do food logos need a symbol, a mascot or a name?

Most do not need a symbol. 34 of the 50 are lettering only, 16 have a symbol or icon, and only 3 have a mascot or character: Hardee's and Carl's Jr. share a smiling star, and Panda Express has a panda.

What the logos of the 50 largest restaurant chains are made of, number of chains

**What the logos of the 50 largest restaurant chains are made of, number of chains**

| Category | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Lettering only | 34 |
| Symbol or icon | 16 |
| Mascot or character | 3 |

*A mascot is also a symbol, so the 3 mascots are counted inside the 16 symbols. The first two bars add up to 50. Source: N&D Co. survey of 50 restaurant chain logos, 7 November 2026.*

The name is almost always in the logo. 47 of 50 spell it out. Only three do not: McDonald's relies on its arches, Dairy Queen on the letters DQ, and Vkusno i Tochka on a symbol. Each of those has years of advertising behind it. A new cafe does not.

Lettering style is where chains differ most, and they differ evenly:

**Lettering style in the logos of the 50 largest restaurant chains**

| Style | Chains |
| --- | --- |
| Bold sans serif | 12 |
| Serif | 12 |
| Script or handwritten | 12 |
| Rounded sans serif | 7 |
| Mixed styles | 5 |
| Regular sans serif | 1 |
| No lettering | 1 |

So the restaurant logotype is a free choice. Pick the style that fits your food and your street, then check that it still reads when small.

## What does this mean for a small food business?

Four things follow from the numbers, and each one is a decision you can make.

First, keep the logo plain. Chains that serve millions of customers a day use one or two colours and, mostly, their name. A cafe logo or eatery logo that carries a drawing, three colours and a slogan has more to lose when it is shrunk onto a delivery app tile.

Second, put your name in it. With 47 of 50 spelling it out, the name is the logo. If a stranger sees it on a delivery app or a sign for two seconds, the name is what they can search for later.

Third, treat red as a choice, not a given. It is the most common colour here, so in a food court of red signs a different main colour may be what makes you stand out. That is our reasoning, not a finding of the study: the data shows only what chains use.

Fourth, a symbol or mascot is optional. Only 16 chains have a symbol and 3 have a mascot. If you add one, it should have a job, such as showing what you sell, and it should survive in one colour.

### A foodie logo with a character

### A pub logo with brush lettering

The [North East logo](https://ndcompany.in/projects/north-east-pub-and-bar) for a pub and bar in Jaipur sets the name in a red-orange brush script that arches over two beer mugs clinking, with PUB and BAR in thin capitals underneath. It has no frame or badge, so it stands on a sign or a menu on its own. Script lettering is one of the three most common styles in the chain data (12 of 50), and the thin capitals under it keep the descriptor readable.

## A checklist before you approve a food logo

This is the practical part. Run every logo option through these tests before you pay for the final files.

1. Shrink it to the size of a delivery app icon. Put it on your phone, or on a small square on screen, and look from arm's length. Can you still read the name? Fine lines, long taglines and small dates are the first things to disappear.
2. Check how it looks on a sign. Hold a printout at the distance a passer-by would see your shopfront from. Thin script and light colours lose out here.
3. Ask for a one-colour version. Receipts, stamps, cups, carry bags and some printers use a single colour. If the logo only works in full colour, you will end up with a worse copy every time.
4. Try it on both a light and a dark background. Menus, windows and social profiles use both.
5. Ask for a compact version. A wide logo is hard to read in the round or square space that apps and social profiles give you. Many identities include an icon or a monogram for that.
6. Check the name against what you sell. Customers should be able to tell if it is a cafe, a restaurant or a bar. Words like Coffee Shop or Pub and Bar, as on two of our examples, do this job.
7. Search for the name before you commit. A look-alike logo or name in your area causes trouble later. If it matters to you, check with a lawyer about trade marks.

### What files to ask for

You need a vector master, so the logo prints sharp on a signboard and a business card, plus PNG files with a transparent background for the web and apps. When we handed over the Sea Seven Fish identity, its logo folders held 59 image files for four versions of one logo. Our guide to [logo file types](https://ndcompany.in/blogs/logo-file-types) explains each format and where to use it. Keep the files in a folder you own, not only in an email thread.

## Is a logo enough, or do you need a brand identity?

A logo is one part of how a food business looks. The colours, fonts and rules for using them decide whether your menu, signboard, packaging and social posts look like the same business. We explain the difference in [logo vs brand identity](https://ndcompany.in/blogs/logo-vs-brand-identity). If you have one location and few menus, a logo with a small colour and font guide may be enough. If you are opening more outlets, selling through apps or printing packaging, an identity saves you from deciding the same things again each time.
